Training Options

Choose Your Path

Every student's situation is different. Here are the three most common training paths — we'll talk through which is right for you.

Most Popular

Sport Pilot Certificate

The lightest regulatory path to a gyroplane certificate. No medical required — a valid driver's license is sufficient. You'll learn to fly during daytime VFR conditions and will be legal to carry one passenger.

Est. Hours 20–40 flight hours
What you can fly Light sport aircraft, daytime VFR
Medical required Valid driver's license

Best for: First-time pilots, recreational flyers who want to keep things simple

Full Certificate

Private Pilot — Gyroplane

The full private pilot certificate with gyroplane as your aircraft category. More flexibility on where and when you can fly, and a foundation for additional ratings down the road.

Est. Hours 40+ flight hours minimum
What you can fly Greater flexibility, passengers, some night VFR
Medical required FAA 3rd Class Medical

Best for: Serious pilots, those planning to own an aircraft and fly frequently

Transition

Transition Training

Already hold a fixed-wing or helicopter certificate? Transition training lets you add gyroplane to your existing certificate. Hours vary based on your background — fixed-wing pilots tend to adapt quickly.

Est. Hours Typically 10–20 hrs (varies)
What you get Gyroplane added to existing certificate
Prerequisite Existing pilot certificate

Best for: Current pilots expanding their ratings and aircraft options

The Curriculum

Phase by Phase — What You'll Learn

Training follows the FAA Practical Test Standards for gyroplane. Here's how it breaks down from first lesson to checkride.

1 Ground & Preflight

Phase 1: Ground School & Preflight

  • Gyroplane aerodynamics — how the rotor works, why it can't stall, what autorotation means for you
  • Aircraft systems — engine, rotor, flight controls, fuel, instruments
  • Aviation weather interpretation and go/no-go decision making
  • FAA regulations applicable to gyroplane operations
  • Preflight inspection: every item on the aircraft, why it matters, what you're looking for
  • Cockpit familiarization, checklists, and emergency procedures on the ground
2 Basic Maneuvers

Phase 2: Basic Flight Maneuvers

  • Rotor pre-rotation and takeoff procedure
  • Climbs, straight-and-level flight, coordinated turns
  • Descents and controlled approach to landing
  • Normal landings and go-arounds
  • Traffic pattern work — entry, downwind, base, final
  • Low-speed flight and recognition of power-off descent characteristics
3 Solo Prep

Phase 3: Solo Preparation

  • Emergency procedure drills: engine-out, forced landing, partial power
  • Autorotation practice — the gyroplane's inherent safety feature
  • Short-field and soft-field takeoff and landing techniques
  • Pre-solo written exam and oral evaluation
  • First solo flight — a milestone you'll remember
  • Post-solo currency building and refining consistency
4 Checkride Prep

Phase 4: Cross-Country & Checkride Preparation

  • VFR navigation, sectional charts, and cross-country flight planning
  • Dual and solo cross-country flights building to required distance thresholds
  • Oral exam preparation — we cover every ACS/PTS topic until you can teach it
  • Practical Test Standards (PTS) maneuver review to checkride tolerances
  • Mock checkride with full debrief before scheduling the real thing
  • Coordination with DPE (designated pilot examiner) for checkride scheduling

FAQ

Training Questions

Zero experience required to start. Most of my students have never touched an aircraft control before their first lesson, and that's fine — in some ways it's easier because you're not unlearning habits from another aircraft type. We'll cover everything from the ground up, starting with how a gyroplane flies before we ever start the engine.
The FAA minimum is 20 flight hours, but most students take somewhere between 25 and 40 hours to reach checkride readiness. The pace depends on how often you can fly, how quickly skills solidify for you, and weather. Flying consistently — even two or three times a week — gets you there much faster than once-a-month sessions. I never push students to the checkride before they're genuinely ready.
You train on my aircraft — you don't need to own anything to earn your certificate. Once you have your certificate, if you decide you want your own Magni, that's a conversation we can have. But plenty of students train, get their certificate, and fly with me on a rental basis for years. Aircraft ownership is a separate decision from learning to fly.
Transition training is a great path for fixed-wing pilots. You'll have credit for some of your aeronautical knowledge and general airmanship, but the gyroplane has its own flight characteristics that you need to experience firsthand — rotor pre-rotation, power-off descent behavior, and landing technique are all different enough that you need dedicated instruction. Most fixed-wing pilots are surprised by how quickly they adapt. Helicopter pilots take a bit more un-learning, but they get there too.
Ground instruction is part of every training program — you can't fly well without understanding what's happening and why. We cover aerodynamics, systems, weather, regulations, and navigation. I can work through ground lessons in person before or after flight lessons, or we can schedule dedicated ground sessions. I'll also point you toward good self-study materials so you're not paying me to read regulations out loud.
The checkride has two parts: an oral exam and a practical (flight) test, both administered by an FAA Designated Pilot Examiner. The oral covers everything from regulations to weather to aerodynamics — it's a conversation, not a multiple-choice test. The practical covers maneuvers from the PTS: takeoffs, landings, turns, emergency procedures, and more. We'll do a full mock checkride before you schedule the real thing so there are no surprises. The goal is to walk in there confident, not hoping for the best.
