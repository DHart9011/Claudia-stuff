The Experience

Here's exactly what happens

From the moment you pull up to the moment you land, here's your intro flight from start to finish.

1

Pre-Flight Briefing on the Ground

Before we ever start the engine, Matt walks you through how a gyroplane actually flies — the rotor, the controls, what to expect in the air. This isn't a boring lecture. It's a 10-minute conversation so nothing surprises you once we're up.

2

You Climb In and We Taxi Out

You'll sit in the front seat of a Magni gyroplane with Matt right behind you. He handles the radio and ground communications while you get used to the cockpit. Then we taxi to the runway and spool up the rotor — that's when it gets real.

3

15–20 Minutes of Actual Flying

Once airborne, Matt may hand you the controls so you can feel how responsive and stable a gyroplane is. You'll see the West Texas landscape like you never have before — wide open, no traffic, just sky. It's genuinely hard to describe until you do it.

4

Land, Debrief, and Ask Everything

After we land, Matt debriefs you on what you saw and felt. This is your chance to ask every question you've had about gyroplanes, training, or ownership. No pressure, no sales pitch — just straight answers from someone who flies these things every week.

The whole point of the intro flight is to let you feel what a gyroplane is like before you decide whether you want to pursue training. You're there to experience it — I handle all the flying unless you want to take the controls, and even then I'm right behind you the whole time.
Gyroplanes are among the most inherently stable aircraft available. The freewheeling rotor can't stall the way a fixed-wing can, and if the engine quits, the rotor keeps spinning and you descend in a controlled autorotation — no different than a normal landing in terms of what the pilot does. That said, like any aircraft, proper training and sound judgment matter. On your intro flight, you'll be flying with an FAA-certified CFI who's done this hundreds of times.
Closed-toe shoes are required — no sandals or flip-flops. Comfortable clothes you can move in, and sunglasses are a good idea. Avoid loose scarves or anything that could catch in the rotor area. West Texas sun is no joke, so if we're flying in summer, a hat that fits under a headset is handy too.
